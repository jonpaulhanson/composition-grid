The Golden Triangle: Composition for Diagonals

By Jon · July 2026 · ~6 min read

When your subject runs on a diagonal — a road cutting across a landscape, a reclining figure, a gesture that travels corner to corner — a horizontal-and-vertical grid fights it. The golden triangle is built for exactly that.

What the golden triangle is

The construction is simple. Run one diagonal corner to corner. Then, from each of the other two corners, drop a line that meets that diagonal at a right angle. You end up with the main diagonal plus two shorter perpendiculars, carving the frame into a set of interlocking right triangles.

Compose your subject along the diagonal, and drop your accents where the two perpendiculars strike it. Those feet are the golden triangle's version of the rule of thirds' power points — the spots that feel anchored rather than accidental.

Four orientations

The diagonal can run from any of the four corners, and each has a mirror version — so there are four distinct orientations to choose from. Pick the one whose diagonal follows your subject's direction of travel, then flip or rotate to fine-tune.

Why perpendiculars, not a ratio

Because it's built from a true perpendicular construction rather than a fixed number, the golden triangle adapts cleanly to any aspect ratio — a square, a tall portrait, a wide panorama all get a correct version. That's a real advantage over the golden spiral, which needs a frame that stays reasonably close to square. Wherever your diagonal runs, the golden triangle can fit it.

One naming note, since it trips people up: online, "golden triangle" gets used for two different constructions. This app uses the true-perpendicular version (the one Wikipedia calls the golden triangle) — where the side lines meet the diagonal at an exact right angle — rather than the looser variant that just connects corners to the diagonal's thirds.

When and how to use it

Reach for it whenever a picture has a strong diagonal thrust. It shows up far less often than thirds or the golden section, which is part of its appeal — it produces an aggressively dynamic flow that doesn't look like everyone else's shot. In practice:

  • Lay your main subject or leading line along the diagonal.
  • Place secondary accents where the perpendiculars strike it.
  • Flip and rotate until the diagonal runs with your subject, not against it.

As always, the honest way in is to see it first — drop the Golden Triangle overlay on a picture you already like and check where your strong diagonal lands.
